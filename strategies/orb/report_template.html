Opening Range Breakout on BTC

Break the high or low of the first N minutes after a chosen session opens. Ride it to a stop, an R multiple, or the session close. Tested to destruction across the full parameter space.

Rejected — does not clear the gate
MECHANISM

Why an edge should exist — and who pays for it

The published ORB results are real, and they are not about the price pattern.

Zarattini, Barbon & Aziz (2024) tested a 5-minute opening range on 7,000+ US stocks, 2016–2023. Unfiltered, it returned +29% total, 41.4% win rate, Sharpe 0.48 — close to nothing. Filtered to the top 20 “stocks in play” by opening relative volume, the same rules returned +1,637%, Sharpe 2.81.

The stated mechanism is that overnight news forces institutions to reprice a stock, and that repricing is compressed into the first minutes of a single daily auction. The opening range is a proxy for an informed participant working a large order right now. The 56× gap between the filtered and unfiltered versions is the whole story: the relative-volume filter is the edge, the breakout is only the trigger.

What breaks on BTC

The counterparty in the equity version is a market maker who must reprice against informed flow at a once-a-day auction. Bitcoin has no auction and no single open; order flow is spread across 24 hours, there is no overnight backlog released at 09:30, no news gap pinned to one clock time, and the book is continuously arbitraged across dozens of venues. The imbalance the papers harvest has nowhere to accumulate.

What is left is “price broke a recent high, buy it” — a generic momentum trigger that pays a round-trip fee on every attempt. That is the thing tested below.

Prior evidence, from your own earlier repo. Session-anchored ORB already failed there on BTC and on real Gold, Silver and Nasdaq futures, every timeframe, both the NY and London sessions. A real London/NY clock scored PF 0.986 and did not beat a plain UTC anchor. Breakout+retest failed at every timeframe; its inverse is what worked. This run is a re-examination with a different exit model, an explicit relative-volume test, and a fade variant in the grid.

TEST 1

The full parameter space, in sample

Every combination of session anchor (8 UTC hours), range length (15m–4h), holding horizon (2h–24h), entry style (stop at the edge vs close beyond it), stop placement (far side of the range, range midpoint, 1× or 2× ATR), target (session close, or 1R–3R), and direction (follow the break or fade it). Each config is scored at four cost levels.

The zero-cost run is the diagnostic that matters

If the distribution cleared 1.0 at zero cost and only failed once fees were charged, ORB would be a fee problem — worth chasing with maker fills or a cheaper venue. It does not. The median configuration is below breakeven before a single basis point is charged, which means the pattern has no edge to protect.

TEST 2

Can a wider stop outrun the cost?

The only configs with a positive gross edge are short fades with a tight ATR stop. Their 1R is small, so a fixed fee is enormous relative to it. The obvious escape is to widen the stop until the fee shrinks as a fraction of R.

The escape does not work: gross edge decays at the same rate the cost burden falls. Net profit factor never approaches 1.0 at any stop width, and the maker column — which assumes a resting limit fill that this entry style cannot guarantee — still tops out at 0.93.

TEST 3

Out of sample: 2024 to now

The identical grid replayed on data no parameter ever saw. The interesting number is not the out-of-sample profit factor on its own — it is what happens to the configurations that topped the in-sample ranking.

TEST 4

The filters the literature says carry the edge

Stage 1 tests the bare pattern. This stage tests the parts the papers actually credit: an opening-range relative volume floor, the QQQ paper's first-candle-direction entry, its stop sized as a fraction of the 14-day ATR, and far targets up to 10R.

Read the two columns together. As the volume floor rises, the best profit factor climbs and the median falls, while the trade count collapses. That is the signature of a statistic getting noisier, not of a filter finding signal — picking the maximum of a noisier sample gets you a bigger maximum for free.

TEST 5

Walk-forward — choosing blind, every quarter

Train on the trailing 12 months, take the best configuration by profit factor, trade it for the next quarter, roll forward. Nothing is ever chosen with knowledge of the quarter it is traded in. This is the test a single top-of-the-grid number cannot fake.

TEST 6

Put it through a prop challenge

Start a fresh account on every trading day. Fixed 1% risk per trade, 4% daily loss, 8% max loss, 8% profit target, real breaches, no size shrinking. If it fails, it fails.

TEST 7

Engine cross-check

The sweep runs on a custom kernel. To be sure the negative result is not an artefact of my own code, the best in-sample configuration was re-run through NautilusTrader on 2022 with real order objects, a margin account on the perpetual, resting stop entries and reduce-only exits.

The two engines agree on trade count to within 7%. NautilusTrader — the more realistic of the two, with genuine order matching — reports the worse number. The kernel is optimistic, not pessimistic, so the null result is not an artefact of it.

VERDICT

Where this leaves ORB

ORB does not clear the gate on BTCUSDT and is not worth building further in its current form. Not one of the configurations tested reaches profit factor 1.20 at a realistic cost, in sample or out. The median configuration loses money before fees are charged at all.

What would change the answer

The literature is clear that the edge lives in the participation filter, not the breakout. On BTC the equivalent of “stocks in play” is not candle volume — it is a data feed: funding rate extremes, open-interest jumps, taker-delta imbalance, long/short ratio, spot-perp basis. Your earlier repo found the same thing independently: every leg that ever worked came from a feed, not from a price pattern. An ORB trigger gated on one of those feeds is a different hypothesis and would need its own test.

Honest weaknesses in this test

  • One symbol. BTCUSDT only, per the current project scope.
  • 15-minute bars, so intrabar path is unknown. Where a bar contains both the stop and the target, the stop is assumed to fill first — pessimistic, but it is an assumption.
  • Relative volume is measured on 15m bars against a 20-day trailing mean. A tick or order-book measure of participation would be sharper and is not available here.
  • The cost model is an assumption — no venue is chosen yet. That is why every result is reported at 0×, 1×, 2× and 3×.
  • A resting-limit (maker) version of the fade was priced but not modelled with queue priority. Your earlier repo has a strategy that backtested at PF 3.0 and traded live at 0.7 for exactly this reason, so that column is a diagnostic, not a result.
